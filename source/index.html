Four ways into any topic

Click a topic and you can read about it at two depths, or work a case about it at two depths. The explainers teach the idea; the cases make you use it under conditions where the answer is not obvious.

Exp20
Working knowledge. Enough to use the idea correctly next week, plus the nuances that trip up newcomers and the questions worth asking before you rely on it.
Exp40
Near-expert depth. The schools of thought and what exactly they disagree on, the hard edge cases, how it differs across product types and company stages, and the diagnostics practitioners actually use.
Case20
A decision case. A protagonist, a deadline, exhibits with real numbers, three distinct options, and a short teaching note showing one defensible path.
Case40
A case with no clear path. Conflicting exhibits, stakeholders with something they are not saying, and a framing that may itself be wrong. It does not resolve, and there is no teaching note — you have to work it out.

Every case is set in India: a fictional company modelled on real Indian firms, with real brands, regulators and institutions as market context, money in lakh and crore, and a team spread across Bengaluru, Pune and offices abroad, so that time zones and markets bear on the decision.

Two AI threads, and an AI lens over everything else

AI shows up in this map three times over, because it changes product work in three different ways.

The first thread, AI is changing the craft, follows what has happened to the job itself now that building is cheap and judgement is not — discovery when synthesis costs nothing, the prototype as the new specification, reviewing work you did not write, where the bottleneck actually moved, and what all of that does to teams, pricing, moats and careers. The second, Building AI products, is product management for products where a model is the product: problem–model fit, evaluation and golden sets, the agent stack, cost and latency, trust and safety, and operating a model in production. Either thread can be followed in reading order from the toolbar.

Underneath both threads sits an AI vocabulary primer — 32 topics defining the words that get used in the room: tokens and context windows, embeddings and vector search, fine-tuning against LoRA and quantisation, RAG and chunking, agents and harnesses, evals and golden sets, what SOTA on a leaderboard does and does not mean. Each one says what the term is, where the industry uses it loosely, and which decision it bears on.

The AI lens is the third. Switch it on and every module in the graph is shaded by how much that practice has genuinely changed — and the point of it is the modules that come back pale. Experimentation and causal inference have barely moved; the statistics are the statistics. Hardware, CPG and portfolio decisions are constrained by physics, money and politics, not by what a model can generate. Qualitative research, prototyping, specification and analytics have changed a great deal. A map where everything glowed would tell you nothing, so this one does not.

Reading it offline. Unzip the folder and open index.html — everything works from disk, with no server and no account. One browser quirk: when opened straight from a folder, topic pages open in a new tab rather than inside the reader, because browsers refuse to load a local file inside a frame.
Coverage so far. 36 modules carry pre-written reading pages — the largest are Product Metrics & Measurement (28), Product Analytics & Data Practice (26), Delivery Process & Engineering Collaboration (23), Prioritisation & Decision-Making (23), Experimentation & Causal Inference (21), Roadmapping, Planning & OKRs (19), Technical Fluency (19), Communication & Stakeholder Management (18) and AI & ML Product Management (17) — 1,715 pages across 472 topics, every one of them shipped inside this site; 376 of those topics sit in the Beginner edition. Every page in this build passes an automated arithmetic check on its tables, totals and percentages. Every other topic opens an editable version of the prompt that would have written it, so you can run it in your own Claude account.

Three traits, and what this map can't give you

Everything else here is drawn from how product management is taught. This part is not — it is a personal view, and it is signed.

High Agency
An internal locus of control. Great PMs fix systemic incentives rather than accepting blockers as fate.
Product Sense
Intuition, not just optimisation. Great PMs sense what users will want before the data proves it.
Curiosity & Empathy
Intellectual curiosity and contextual empathy. Always spinning threads on why things are the way they are — pointed at systems and at people, who are understood inside their real circumstances rather than as an abstraction invented at a desk.

Switch the pillar lens on inside either graph and the three shade the map differently. Product sense and curiosity each cover about twenty modules and four hundred topics. Agency covers fourteen and roughly two hundred and seventy — and those modules teach you about agency rather than building it. That is not a gap in the map; it is a fact about the trait. Use the map for two of the three, and find yourself a situation for the third.

Where this came from

This was built for the Software Product Management course at the Indian School of Business, and is used with students there. The course covers a deliberately small part of this map; the rest is here so students can see what lies beyond the syllabus and go there on their own.

The structure — lesson-sized topics, explicit prerequisites, and a topic that stays locked until its prerequisites make sense — is borrowed from Math Academy's knowledge graph. The module coverage, the four teaching lenses and the sequencing pathways draw on a 2026 benchmark of fifty product management programmes at leading business and technology schools.

How these pages were made. The explainers and case studies were generated with Claude from a curated set of prompts, then checked for structure, length and arithmetic before publication. The companies, people and figures in the cases are fictional, invented to make a teaching point; where real firms, brands and regulators appear, they are market context only and nothing discreditable is attributed to them. Nothing here is investment, legal or professional advice, and no page should be treated as a factual account of any real company.
